Medium Mixed-Breed Dog: healthy weight, feeding chart and care

A medium mixed-breed dog is healthy at 24–55 lb (11–25 kg) as an adult and typically lives 11–14 years. At 40 lb, a spayed or neutered adult needs about 985 kcal a day, roughly 2.74 cups of a typical dry food, and 60–90 minutes of exercise.

Healthy weight

The healthy adult range is 24–55 lb (11–25 kg). Mixed-breed dogs vary a lot, so treat the range as a guide and judge by body condition instead of the scale alone.

Ribs felt without pressing. Waist visible behind the ribs, abdomen tucked. This is the target. That is a body condition score of 5 on the 9-point scale vets use. See all nine scores and find where your dog sits.

A puppy of this size is still growing until about 1 year. Until then, weigh weekly and follow the trend with your vet instead of aiming at an adult number.

What Mixed breeds are prone to

A predisposition is not a diagnosis. Most Mixed breeds never develop most of these. Knowing the list tells you what to screen for and what to raise with your vet.

  • Hip dysplasiaScreening available

    Loose hip joints that grind into arthritis. Ask about OFA or PennHIP screening; keeping them lean is the single biggest lever you control.

  • Cruciate ligament ruptureWatch for signs

    A knee ligament tear causing sudden hind-limb lameness. Weight control and steady conditioning lower the risk.

  • ObesityWatch for signs

    The most common preventable disease in pets. Weigh food, count treats, and re-score body condition monthly.

  • Dental diseaseWatch for signs

    Most pets have periodontal disease by age three. Daily brushing plus professional cleaning under anesthesia is what actually works.

  • Atopic dermatitisWatch for signs

    Environmental allergy causing itchy feet, ears, and belly. Modern treatments are far better than steroids.

  • How to use this listWatch for signs

    These are breed tendencies drawn from population data, not findings in your animal. Most dogs and cats of any breed never develop most of these. Bring the list to your next appointment and ask which two are worth screening for at this age.

Go to a vet now if you see this

  • Trouble breathing, open-mouth breathing in a cat, or gums that are blue, white or brick red
  • Repeated unproductive retching, a swollen or drum-tight belly, restlessness and pacing — this is bloat until proven otherwise
  • Collapse, sudden weakness, or a seizure lasting over two minutes or repeating
  • Bleeding that will not stop, or a suspected broken bone
  • Suspected poisoning: chocolate, xylitol, grapes, lilies for cats, antifreeze, human medication
  • Sudden inability to use the hind legs, or crying out when touched along the back

Common questions

How much should a medium mixed-breed dog weigh?

A healthy adult medium mixed-breed dog usually weighs 24–55 lb (11–25 kg). The number matters less than body condition: you should feel the ribs without pressing and see a waist from above.

How much should I feed a medium mixed-breed dog?

A spayed or neutered adult at 40 lb needs about 985 kcal a day, which is roughly 2.74 cups of a 360 kcal-per-cup food split into 2 meals. Check the calories per cup on your own bag, because foods range from under 300 to over 450.

How long do Mixed breeds live?

Typically 11–14 years. They count as senior from about age 9, when twice-yearly vet visits start to pay off.

How much exercise does a medium mixed-breed dog need?

About 60–90 minutes a day for a healthy adult. Sniffing walks tire a dog more than distance. Let them stop.

What health problems are Mixed breeds prone to?

The ones to know about are hip dysplasia, cruciate ligament rupture, obesity, dental disease, atopic dermatitis and how to use this list. A predisposition is not a diagnosis, but it tells you what to screen for and what to mention to your vet.

Sources and method

These figures are computed, not written by hand, from Dewclaw's breed data. How we calculate has the formulas and every factor.

  • Energy needs: resting energy requirement, 70 × (kg)0.75, times a life-stage factor, as in the WSAVA Global Nutrition Guidelines.
  • Body condition: the WSAVA 9-point body condition score.
  • Life stages: AAHA canine and AAHA/AAFP feline life stage guidelines.
  • Weight range and lifespan: breed standard ranges and published breed lifespans.

Dewclaw is not a veterinarian. These figures are computed from published general-population formulas and breed tendencies. They are a starting point for a conversation with your vet, not a diagnosis or a treatment plan. Health conditions, medication, pregnancy and nursing all change them.
