Exotic Shorthair: healthy weight, feeding chart and care

An Exotic Shorthair is healthy at 7–13 lb (3–6 kg) as an adult and typically lives 12–15 years. At 10 lb, a spayed or neutered adult needs about 261 kcal a day, roughly 0.73 cups of a typical dry food, and 2–3 sessions of 8–12 minutes of play.

Healthy Exotic Shorthair weight

The healthy adult range is 7–13 lb (3–6 kg). Individual frames differ, so treat the range as a guide and judge by body condition instead of the scale alone.

Ribs felt without pressing. Waist visible behind the ribs, abdomen tucked. This is the target. That is a body condition score of 5 on the 9-point scale vets use. See all nine scores and find where your cat sits.

A kitten of this breed is still growing until about 1 year. Until then, weigh weekly and follow the trend with your vet instead of aiming at an adult number.

What Exotic Shorthairs are prone to

A predisposition is not a diagnosis. Most Exotic Shorthairs never develop most of these. Knowing the list tells you what to screen for and what to raise with your vet.

  • Brachycephalic airway syndromeWatch for signs

    A short skull with normal-length soft tissue means every breath is work. Avoid heat and exertion; loud snoring and exercise intolerance are surgical red flags, not personality.

  • Polycystic kidney diseaseScreening available

    Inherited fluid cysts in the kidneys. A DNA test exists; ultrasound confirms.

  • Dental diseaseWatch for signs

    Most pets have periodontal disease by age three. Daily brushing plus professional cleaning under anesthesia is what actually works.

  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathyScreening available

    The most common feline heart disease; the muscle thickens silently. Ask about screening echo, especially before anesthesia.

  • ObesityWatch for signs

    The most common preventable disease in pets. Weigh food, count treats, and re-score body condition monthly.

  • How to use this listWatch for signs

    These are breed tendencies drawn from population data, not findings in your animal. Most dogs and cats of any breed never develop most of these. Bring the list to your next appointment and ask which two are worth screening for at this age.

Go to a vet now if you see this

  • Trouble breathing, open-mouth breathing in a cat, or gums that are blue, white or brick red
  • Repeated unproductive retching, a swollen or drum-tight belly, restlessness and pacing
  • Collapse, sudden weakness, or a seizure lasting over two minutes or repeating
  • Straining in the litter box or unable to pass urine — in male cats this is fatal within a day
  • Bleeding that will not stop, or a suspected broken bone
  • Suspected poisoning: chocolate, xylitol, grapes, lilies for cats, antifreeze, human medication

Common questions

How much should a Exotic Shorthair weigh?

A healthy adult Exotic Shorthair usually weighs 7–13 lb (3–6 kg). The number matters less than body condition: you should feel the ribs without pressing and see a waist from above.

How much should I feed a Exotic Shorthair?

A spayed or neutered adult at 10 lb needs about 261 kcal a day, which is roughly 0.73 cups of a 360 kcal-per-cup food split into 3 meals. Check the calories per cup on your own bag, because foods range from under 300 to over 450.

How long do Exotic Shorthairs live?

Typically 12–15 years. They count as senior from about age 11, when twice-yearly vet visits start to pay off.

How much play does a Exotic Shorthair need?

About 2–3 sessions of 8–12 minutes a day for a healthy adult. End each session with a catch and a meal — hunt, catch, eat, groom, sleep is the sequence cats are built around.

What health problems are Exotic Shorthairs prone to?

The ones to know about are brachycephalic airway syndrome, polycystic kidney disease, dental disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, obesity and how to use this list. A predisposition is not a diagnosis, but it tells you what to screen for and what to mention to your vet.
